Automated Compromise Response System

A hardware gate that keeps automated cyber-defense safe.

Automated defense can cut a compromised device off the network in milliseconds. On a hospital network, cutting the wrong device darkens a patient monitor. ACRS sits between the two: fast automation still acts, and no command reaches a life-critical device until it has passed every check.

The problem

Speed without a safety interlock

Automated defense tools (SOAR/XDR — software that acts on attacks by itself) are built for ordinary IT, where isolating a laptop by mistake costs an hour. On life-critical networks — hospitals and industrial control systems — an automated mistake has a physical cost.

What automation lacks

No physical fail-safe, and no human gate for the few actions that must never be wrong.

What ACRS adds

A physical and cryptographic gate: forged commands are refused, and a life-critical device is only disconnected after two people approve on the device itself.

How it works

Six checks, in order, on every command

  1. SignatureThe command is signed with HMAC-SHA256. A forged one is refused.
  2. ReplayEach command carries a one-time number. A reused one is refused.
  3. SourceOnly a known, verified sender is accepted.
  4. PolicyThe gate decides how critical the target is — never the request.
  5. Two-personCritical targets need two different people and two PINs on the keypad.
  6. RelayOnly then does the gate act. Every decision is written to a signed log.
Routine

Isolated automatically, in milliseconds.

Critical

Held until two people approve with two PINs.

Never-isolate

Refused even when the command is correctly signed.

Results

51 ms from detection to cut-off

Measured on the gate itself, from the detection event to the attacker being cut off, over 10 repeated attacks. The fastest run took 41 ms and the slowest 59 ms.

On the device

Every device on its own network, ranked by risk

The gate runs its own isolated Wi-Fi network, ACRS-NET. Decoy honeypot ports and an SSH brute-force watch detect attackers, and a firewall enforces containment. Cut-off and unverified devices are listed first.

Design details

Why each check is hard to get around

Freshness without clocks

Every command must carry a single-use number issued by the gate itself. A replayed or stale command fails, and nothing depends on the sender's clock.

Default deny

The gate holds its own lists of critical and never-isolate devices. How critical a target is, is never taken from the request.

Approvals only at the device

Two different approvers type their PINs on the physical keypad. PINs are stored as salted PBKDF2 hashes, and an approval arriving over the network is ignored.

Lockout

Three alerts within 60 seconds, or PIN guessing, lock the gate for 2 minutes. While locked, even a valid request is refused.

A log that proves itself

Each record is chained to the one before it with HMAC. Changing or deleting any record breaks the chain, and the gate shows it.

Decoys on the network

Five decoy ports (FTP, Telnet, SMB, RDP, VNC) have no real service behind them. Touching one is an alert; touching three is treated as a port scan.

Threat model

What the gate detects, and how it labels it

Each alert is tagged with the matching MITRE ATT&CK technique, so an incident can be read in a standard vocabulary.

  • Forged or malformed commandBad signature or broken requestT0855
  • Replayed commandReused or stale one-time numberT0855
  • Approval sent over the networkIgnored: approvals count only at the keypadT0855
  • Unverified sourceA sender the gate does not know issuing commandsT0848
  • Decoy port touchedSomeone probing the networkT0846
  • Port scanThree or more decoys from one addressT1046
  • PIN guessing / SSH brute forceRepeated wrong credentialsT1110.001
  • Same approver twiceOne person trying to act as twoT1110
  • ARP spoofing / address takeoverA device claiming another's addressT1557

Limits of this work

What has not been shown yet

  • ACRS is a working prototype. It has been tested on its own network, not in a hospital or on real medical devices.
  • The 51 ms figure comes from 10 runs on one device. A larger sample on different hardware is needed before generalising.
  • Signing keys are stored on the device. Moving them into a hardware security module is planned.
  • The lists of critical and never-isolate devices are set by hand. Linking them to a hospital asset inventory is planned.
